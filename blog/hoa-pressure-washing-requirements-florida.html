📋 HOA Compliance

HOA Pressure Washing Requirements in Florida — What to Know

If you live in an HOA community in Miami-Dade or Broward, exterior cleanliness is almost certainly written into your documents somewhere. Most people only find out when a letter shows up.

This is a practical guide to what associations typically expect and how to stay ahead of it. It isn't legal advice — your own governing documents are what actually binds you, and they're the thing to read.

What Associations Typically Require

Wording varies, but the substance is consistent. Most South Florida HOA documents require owners to maintain the exterior in good condition and appearance, which in practice covers:

Roofs free of visible staining — the black streaking that shows up on tile and shingle across South Florida is the single most commonly cited item.

Driveways and walkways without heavy discolouration — green or black growth, deep oil staining, rust marks from irrigation.

Exterior walls free of mildew — particularly the shaded elevations and anywhere under a soffit line.

Fences and screen enclosures maintained — algae on vinyl and wood, and the grey film that builds on screens.

The standard is usually subjective, something like "clean and well-maintained." That sounds vague, and it is — which in practice means enforcement is driven by whoever complains.

How Enforcement Usually Works

Almost always complaint-driven. A neighbour or a board member notices, it goes to management, and you get a letter.

The typical sequence is a courtesy notice first, then a formal violation notice with a deadline to correct — commonly 14 to 30 days — and then fines if it isn't resolved. Florida law sets out a process associations have to follow before fining, including notice and an opportunity to be heard, but the specifics depend on your association's documents and how they've adopted them.

The practical takeaway: the first letter is not the problem. Ignoring it is. Almost every case that turns into real money started with a notice somebody set aside.

Violations We See Most Often

In order of how often they come up:

Roof streaking. The most cited, and the most misunderstood — most owners assume it's dirt and that it'll rinse off. It won't. It's biological.

Driveway discolouration. Particularly shaded driveways and anything under a tree line.

Mildew on stucco. Nearly always on the north-facing or shaded elevation first.

Dirty screen enclosures and pool cages. Frequently overlooked because owners see them from the inside, where the film is much less obvious.

If You've Already Got a Letter

Read the deadline first, and take it at face value. Then get it cleaned well before that date — not on it. Associations generally re-inspect after the deadline, and being visibly finished early tends to close things out quietly.

Photograph the finished work. Date-stamped photos of the corrected condition are worth having if there's ever a dispute about whether or when it was addressed.

If the deadline genuinely isn't achievable, contact management before it passes and say so. Associations are generally far more accommodating about a scheduling conflict raised in advance than a deadline that simply went by.

The Easiest Way to Never Deal With This

Get ahead of the cycle. In this climate, surfaces drift from clean to cited in under a year, and the letter always arrives at an inconvenient moment.

An annual roof soft wash and a twice-yearly driveway and exterior clean keeps most South Florida properties comfortably inside what associations expect. That's exactly what our recurring plans are built around — scheduled visits so the property never drifts far enough to attract attention.

It's also simply cheaper. Correcting a property that's been let go for three years costs considerably more than maintaining one that never got there.
